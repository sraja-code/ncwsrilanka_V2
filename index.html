About Us

History

Sri Lanka has made steady progress in advancing women’s rights, beginning with the introduction of universal adult franchise in 1931, which enabled women’s equal participation in the democratic process. After independence, constitutional guarantees on equality and non-discrimination were strengthened, along with legal reforms covering employment rights, maternity protection, education, and protections against sexual harassment, domestic violence, trafficking, and other forms of gender-based violence. This commitment is also supported through international obligations, including under the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW).

Sri Lanka’s institutional focus on women’s rights gained momentum following the International Women’s Year in 1975 and the UN Decade for Women (1975–1985). The establishment of the Women’s Bureau in 1978 marked an important step in creating a national mechanism for women’s affairs and promoting women’s participation and empowerment.

Following Sri Lanka’s ratification of CEDAW in 1981, the approach increasingly shifted towards a rights-based focus on equality and the elimination of discrimination against women. The Sri Lanka Women’s Charter, adopted in 1993, and the establishment of the National Committee on Women further reflected the recognition of women’s rights and gender equality.

The 1995 Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action marked a shift towards addressing the structural and social factors underlying inequality. Since then, Sri Lanka’s policy framework has increasingly expanded to include concepts such as gender equality, gender mainstreaming, gender-responsive budgeting and sexual and gender-based violence.

Despite this progress, gaps remain in the implementation and enforcement of laws. Women continue to face structural barriers, inconsistent application of laws, limited institutional responsiveness to sexual and gender-based violence, and weak coordination among responsible agencies, which together affect the effectiveness of protections and slow progress toward substantive equality.

In this context, while existing institutions working on women’s rights often operate with sector-specific mandates and limited coordination, the National Commission on Women (NCW), established under the Women Empowerment Act, No. 37 of 2024, provides a dedicated statutory framework with clear authority to address women’s rights in a more integrated and coordinated manner.

The NCW is empowered and authorised to inquire into and investigate infringements or imminent infringements of women’s rights, receive and examine complaints, conduct inquiries where necessary, and facilitate mediation or refer matters to relevant authorities. It is also empowered to intervene in legal proceedings with the permission of court, initiate actions before competent courts in relation to violations of women’s rights, and monitor compliance with relevant laws, policies, and constitutional provisions. Furthermore, the Commission is mandated to review existing laws and programmes, make recommendations for legal and policy reforms, and advise the Government on measures to strengthen protection and equality for women, functioning both as a mechanism for addressing individual grievances and as a national-level institution for systemic reform and accountability.

How to Make a Complaint

An aggrieved person, or anyone acting on her behalf, may apply to the Commission. The application must be made within one month of the infringement, or of becoming aware of it.

After deciding on the complaint, the Commission must inform the aggrieved person of the action taken within 14 days of the decision.
